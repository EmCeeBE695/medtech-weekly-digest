Haemonetics Wins FDA Approval for PerQseal Elite Sutureless Vascular Closure System

New Products/Approvals

Core Development: Haemonetics announced FDA approval (Oct. 20) of PerQseal Elite, a sutureless, fully bioabsorbable vascular closure system that seals large-bore arteriotomies (14–22F sheaths, up to 26F) from inside the vessel, leaving nothing permanent behind. The device came via Haemonetics' January 2026 acquisition of Vivasure Medical, and is approved for closing large-bore arterial access sites after structural heart (e.g., TAVR) and endovascular procedures.

Context & Impact: As transcatheter structural-heart procedures grow, the big sheaths used to deliver them create large arteriotomies that have historically been closed with suture-based devices or surgical cutdown — slower and more complication-prone. A fully resorbable patch that seals from inside in under a minute, with 0% major complications in its pivotal study, could meaningfully cut vascular complication rates and recovery time, a real pain point in cath labs, and lets interventional teams close big holes about as easily as small ones.

Technical & Regulatory: Pivotal ELITE study (prospective, single-arm, multicenter): 0% major complications per VARC-3 criteria, median time to hemostasis under 1 minute. Already has EU CE Mark (since 2025, for both arterial and venous indications); US approval for now covers arterial only. Data will be presented at TCT 2026 (Oct. 31–Nov. 3). Watch for a US venous-indication filing next.

Fresenius Medical Care Launches FDA-Cleared multiFiltratePRO Continuous Kidney Replacement Therapy System

New Products/Approvals
Class connection

Core Development: FDA cleared the multiFiltratePRO system (Oct. 19) and Fresenius Medical Care launched it in the US (Oct. 22) for continuous kidney replacement therapy (CKRT) in critically ill patients with acute kidney injury. Built-in citrate and calcium pumps run the "Ci-Ca Protocol," automatically adjusting anticoagulant and calcium infusion rates as blood/effluent flow changes — eliminating the need for separate external infusion pumps. It's paired with newly FDA-approved Cienby (citrate) and Calrezdy (calcium) solutions.

Context & Impact: Regional citrate anticoagulation (RCA) is gentler on critically ill patients than systemic heparin and extends filter life, but juggling separate pumps for citrate and calcium alongside the CKRT machine is a real workflow and dosing-error risk for ICU nurses. Folding it into one integrated system that auto-adjusts the ratio is exactly the kind of bedside-simplification move that could let more ICUs adopt RCA confidently.

Technical & Regulatory: Indicated for patients ≥40kg with acute renal failure, with or without fluid overload; physician-prescribed. Key safety flags: hypo/hypercalcemia, hypernatremia, hypomagnesemia, metabolic acid-base disturbances (Cienby); arrhythmia/hypotension risk, no co-administration with cardiac glycosides (Calrezdy). Being presented at ASN Kidney Week 2026 (Denver). US rollout starts controlled before going broad.

Sequel Med Tech's Twiist Automated Insulin Delivery System Cleared for Insulin-Treated Type 2 Diabetes

New Products/Approvals
Class connection

Core Development: FDA cleared (disclosed Oct. 16; 510(k) K262576) Sequel Med Tech's Twiist automated insulin delivery (AID) system for adults with insulin-treated Type 2 diabetes, extending a clearance it first won for Type 1 diabetes in 2024. The dosing algorithm, developed with partner DEKA, traces back to Tidepool Loop — the patient-led, open-source "looping" algorithm originally built by the DIY diabetes community. Twiist pairs with CGMs including Abbott's FreeStyle Libre 3 Plus and Senseonics' Eversense 365.

Context & Impact: AID ("artificial pancreas") systems have mostly targeted Type 1 diabetes; Type 2 is a much larger population, and many of those patients have little experience with carb-counting or looping tech. In a 13-week multicenter study, average blood sugar fell from 8.1% to 7.4% and time-in-range rose from 57% to 73%, with many patients improving within 24 hours despite no prior AID experience — a meaningful glycemic-control and quality-of-life upgrade for an underserved population, and it sharpens competition with Beta Bionics, Insulet, MiniMed, and Tandem, all chasing the same Type 2 opportunity.

Technical & Regulatory: 510(k) clearance. Notable regulatory lineage: the underlying algorithm traces to Tidepool Loop, itself FDA-cleared in 2023 as a patient-facing dosing tool — a case of grassroots, patient-hacked technology becoming the technical backbone of a commercially cleared product. Open question: real-world adherence and safety in a population generally less engaged with device-based diabetes management than Type 1 patients.

Stryker Invests $400M in CMR Surgical, Gains Option to Acquire Soft-Tissue Robotics Maker

Tech Innovations

Core Development: Stryker announced (Oct. 12, 2026 — a few days outside the usual 7-day window, included for its significance) a roughly $400 million investment in CMR Surgical, becoming the exclusive US distributor of CMR's Versius soft-tissue surgical robot and gaining an exclusive option to acquire the rest of the company. Versius is a modular robot — it can roll between operating rooms — that's performed more than 45,000 procedures globally and was FDA-cleared last year (as "Versius Plus") for gallbladder surgery in the US.

Context & Impact: Robotic surgery has been Intuitive Surgical's near-monopoly for two decades; now J&J and Medtronic have FDA-authorized robots of their own, and Stryker — which already has orthopedic robotics (Mako) and a large OR-equipment business — didn't want to be the major device maker left without a soft-tissue robot. One analyst called it a "now or never" move; CMR is estimated to be roughly the second- or third-largest soft-tissue robot by global install base, giving Stryker a real foothold rather than starting from zero — though most doubt it unseats da Vinci any time soon. The bigger near-term effect may be squeezing J&J and Medtronic further as a fourth major player enters.

Technical & Regulatory: Versius already has FDA clearance for cholecystectomy (gallbladder) procedures; CMR plans to pursue additional soft-tissue indications. Analysts expect Stryker to initially push Versius into lower-cost settings like ambulatory surgery centers rather than compete head-on with da Vinci in hospitals — a positioning and regulatory-indication-expansion challenge as much as a technical one.

Clinical Advances

Dexcom's CONNECT Study, Published in NEJM, Supports CGM Use in Type 2 Diabetes Patients Not on Insulin

Clinical Advances

Core Development: Results of Dexcom's CONNECT study — a 440-patient randomized trial across 22 US primary-care practices — published Oct. 14 in the New England Journal of Medicine. Adults with Type 2 diabetes not on insulin were randomized to Dexcom's G7 CGM or routine care; at 26 weeks, the CGM group's HbA1c fell from 8.8% to 7.2% (vs. 8.7% to 8.0% with routine care), and they spent roughly five more hours per day in target glucose range.

Context & Impact: This matters for a population — roughly 25 million US adults with Type 2 diabetes not on insulin — that currently gets little to no CGM coverage from Medicare or many insurers, who typically reserve coverage for insulin-treated patients. Peer-reviewed, randomized, NEJM-published evidence is exactly the proof payers look for before expanding coverage, and the benefit held up across age, race, income, and insurance status, undercutting the idea that CGM only helps the most motivated or affluent patients.

Technical & Regulatory: CMS hasn't yet decided whether to expand Medicare CGM coverage to non-insulin Type 2 patients; analysts expect a decision by year-end 2026 with mid-2027 implementation. If expanded, Dexcom's addressable Medicare population could roughly double (from ~15M to ~30M covered lives, per Leerink Partners); Abbott estimates roughly 10 million added Medicare beneficiaries. The remaining hurdle is now a reimbursement/policy decision rather than a clinical one.

FDA Warning Letter Cites Systemic Quality Management Failures at Olympus

Regulatory Updates

Core Development: FDA issued a warning letter (dated Oct. 7, disclosed by Olympus in mid-October) following inspections of six Olympus manufacturing sites in the US, Japan, and Europe in late 2025. The letter describes systemic gaps in how the company designs, monitors, and controls production — not an isolated single-product issue. Olympus is the dominant global endoscope supplier for gastroenterology, pulmonology, and urology.

Context & Impact: This follows a documented pattern: 2022 warning letters, a late-2023 Class I bronchoscope recall tied to a death and burn injuries, a 2024 endoscope-component recall linked to a death and 100+ injuries, and a 2025 needle recall tied to one death. A "systemic" finding — rather than a fixable single part number — is a much bigger deal for a company this central to global endoscopic procedure volume; it implies the fix has to reach the quality organization itself, not just one product line.

Technical & Regulatory: Olympus has 15 business days to respond in detail and must provide periodic updates until FDA accepts its corrective/preventive actions. The company says production, manufacturing, and distribution continue uninterrupted and financial guidance is unchanged — but a separate, pre-existing 2025 import block already restricts certain Japan-made bronchoscopes, laparoscopes, and ureterorenoscopes. Whether this new letter escalates into a broader import alert is the thing to watch next.

Medtronic Completes Full Separation of MiniMed Group

Market Developments

Core Development: Medtronic completed the full separation of MiniMed Group (its insulin-pump/diabetes-tech business) on Oct. 15, 2026, via an exchange offer letting Medtronic shareholders trade Medtronic stock for MiniMed shares. The offer was 5.4x oversubscribed, so Medtronic exchanged its entire remaining stake and now holds zero MiniMed shares. MiniMed, which went public in March 2026, trades on Nasdaq as MMED and closed at $17.50 the day the split completed.

Context & Impact: This is the final step in unwinding one of the largest diabetes-device businesses from its parent, part of a broader medtech trend of conglomerates spinning off focused units (echoing Abbott's recently announced nutrition-business spin-off and Siemens Healthineers narrowing its ultrasound lineup). Full independence gives MiniMed its own capital allocation and strategic decisions — CEO Que Dallara called it newfound "freedom" — but also means navigating a competitive insulin-pump market (Insulet, Tandem, Beta Bionics, and now Sequel in Type 2) without a deep-pocketed parent behind it.

Technical & Regulatory: No device or regulatory change — this is purely a corporate-structure event. Operationally, MiniMed still has roughly 160 technology-service agreements with Medtronic that it's working to exit (expecting to wind down most by 2027), which is a real transition risk for manufacturing/supply continuity worth watching over the next year.

Emerging Trends
💡

Diabetes tech had an unusually newsy week, and the throughline is market expansion into populations that have historically been underserved by advanced glucose management: Dexcom's NEJM-published CONNECT trial builds the clinical case for CGM in Type 2 patients not yet on insulin (roughly 25 million Americans), and Sequel's Twiist clearance extends automated insulin delivery — previously a Type-1-dominated category — to insulin-treated Type 2 patients. Neither is really a technology breakthrough so much as proof that existing, already-validated tech (continuous sensing, closed-loop dosing) works in a bigger population than it was first built for. MiniMed's completed spin-off from Medtronic adds a structural note to the same story: as diabetes tech matures into a large, competitive, strategically important category on its own, its corporate owners are increasingly carving it out as a standalone business rather than running it as one division among many.

Elsewhere, the week's bigger strategic moves were about consolidation and positioning rather than new technology per se. Stryker's $400 million bet on CMR Surgical is the clearest signal yet that soft-tissue surgical robotics is shifting from a one-company (Intuitive) market to a genuine multi-player field, now with four large device makers (Intuitive, J&J, Medtronic, and soon Stryker) competing for OR share. And Olympus's warning letter — following a multi-year pattern of recalls tied to patient deaths and injuries — is a reminder that scale and market dominance don't insulate a company from sustained quality-system scrutiny; if anything, being the default supplier for so many procedures raises the stakes when systemic problems surface.

Taken together: incremental device engineering this week (sutureless closure, integrated infusion pumps) is mostly about removing friction and risk from existing clinical workflows rather than introducing new mechanisms of action — a useful, if less flashy, counterpoint to AI/robotics headlines, and a good illustration that "innovation" in this industry is as often about simplification and recombination of known pieces as it is about invention from scratch.
