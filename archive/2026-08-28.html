New Products/Approvals

FDA Authorizes Abbott's Libre Duo, First Combined Glucose-Ketone Wearable

New Products/Approvals
Class connection

Core Development: On August 25, 2026, the FDA granted Abbott's Libre Duo 10 Day Continuous Dual Glucose Ketone Monitoring System marketing authorization via the De Novo pathway — the first wearable in the U.S., and the first in the world, to continuously track both glucose and ketone levels in a single sensor. Worn on the arm, it samples interstitial fluid every minute and pushes readings to a smartphone app, alerting users when ketone levels climb toward concerning thresholds.

Context & Impact: Existing ketone testing is a single-point-in-time check (a fingerstick or urine strip) — useful only if you happen to test at the right moment. This device turns that into a continuous trend line, so a patient or caregiver can catch a rising ketone trajectory before it becomes diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), a life-threatening emergency and a leading reason people with type 1 diabetes end up in the ER. For a population that already wears a CGM, folding ketone sensing into the same patch removes a whole separate category of testing.

Technical & Regulatory: The De Novo pathway is reserved for novel, low-to-moderate-risk device types with no existing predicate — FDA used it here to simultaneously set new special controls (labeling, performance testing, clinical evidence) that will become the template other ketone-sensing wearables get measured against. Next hurdles are real-world accuracy across a broader population and, per the Medicare coverage story below, payer reimbursement.

Tech Innovations

Philips Details $33.7M ARPA-H-Funded Push Toward Autonomous Thrombectomy

Tech Innovations

Core Development: Philips announced details of a $33.7M ARPA-H (the HHS "DARPA for health") award to develop a robot capable of performing mechanical thrombectomy — the catheter-based clot-removal procedure for ischemic stroke — with increasing autonomy. It builds on Philips' existing Azurion image-guided platform plus a steerable catheter and machine-learning navigation trained through imitation learning, and is part of a larger $175.3M ARPA-H endovascular-robotics effort that also funds Siemens Healthineers.

Context & Impact: Thrombectomy is extremely time-sensitive ("time is brain"), and only a fraction of hospitals have an interventionalist on-site around the clock to perform it — most stroke patients who'd benefit never get transferred fast enough. An autonomous or remotely-supervised robot could let a remote specialist guide (or eventually let software largely execute) the procedure at a hospital without one on staff, closing a real access gap rather than just speeding up an existing one.

Technical & Regulatory: This is early-stage development funding, not a cleared product. Academic partners — Johns Hopkins on autonomous navigation, Boston University on the steerable catheter, Weill Cornell on clinical input — are building toward a validation test bed that Kitware is separately funded ($17.5M) to construct. Expect years of simulation, animal, and eventually human feasibility studies before anything resembling autonomous thrombectomy reaches an FDA submission; full autonomy in a brain-vasculature procedure will draw a very high evidentiary bar.

FDA Expands Device Compatibility for Paradromics' Connexus Brain-Computer Interface

Tech Innovations

Core Development: The FDA authorized an expanded device-compatibility pathway for Paradromics' Connexus brain-computer interface (a Neuralink competitor), letting its Convey system connect to a patient's own laptop, tablet, or phone without a separate FDA submission for every new device added.

Context & Impact: Most BCI trials to date have tethered patients to company-supplied, tightly controlled computing hardware — workable for a clinical trial, but not how anyone actually lives. Letting the same implant talk to whatever phone or tablet a patient already owns (or a wheelchair-mounted device, or a home tablet) is what turns a BCI from a lab demo into something usable day to day, and it signals FDA is willing to regulate the platform rather than re-review every hardware permutation.

Technical & Regulatory: This is a compatibility-pathway clearance, not authorization of Connexus for general marketing — Paradromics' implant itself is still in early feasibility trials (FDA approved a speech-restoration study in late 2025). The precedent matters for neurotech broadly: as BCIs move toward continuous, high-bandwidth data exchange with consumer devices, regulators will need a framework for the whole ecosystem — implant, software, and whatever external hardware a patient plugs in — not just the implanted part.

Regulatory Updates

FDA Early Alert: Abiomed's Automated Impella Controller Purge Flag Failures

Regulatory Updates
Class connection

Core Development: On August 26, 2026, FDA issued an early alert on Abiomed's (J&J) Automated Impella Controller — the touchscreen interface clinicians use to run and monitor the Impella heart pump during cardiogenic shock and high-risk PCI support. A failed purge flag component can throw false "Purge Disc Disconnected" alarms, delaying or interrupting circulatory support; as of August 17, Abiomed had logged 37 serious injuries and 3 deaths tied to the issue.

Context & Impact: Impella pumps support some of the sickest patients in the hospital — anyone whose heart can't move enough blood on its own during a procedure or a shock episode. A controller throwing a false alarm mid-support isn't a paperwork problem; it can mean momentarily losing mechanical circulatory support in a patient with no reserve to spare. FDA's guidance — reinsert the disc, fall back to a backup controller, keep using existing inventory while replacements are scheduled — is a stopgap that keeps units in hospitals rather than pulling them all at once, underscoring how central this device is to cath-lab and ICU workflows.

Technical & Regulatory: This is an early alert rather than a full recall notice, letting facilities keep operating affected units under interim mitigations while Abiomed schedules a purge flag and retainer swap site by site. It's the second Impella-related purge-system issue in six months, following a February 2026 first-generation purge cassette alert — likely to draw added scrutiny to Abiomed's post-market quality processes on this product line.

Boston Scientific's ENROUTE Carotid Catheter Recalled as Cyberattack Disrupts Operations

Regulatory Updates
Class connection

Core Development: Boston Scientific had a rough August 26: FDA classified a recall of its ENROUTE Transcarotid Neuroprotection System catheters as Class I — the most serious category — over reports that the catheter's arterial sheath tip can separate or partially separate during use. Separately, the company disclosed a cyberattack that knocked out order processing and shipping globally.

Context & Impact: ENROUTE is used during transcarotid artery revascularization to catch debris and protect the brain while a surgeon opens a blocked carotid artery — it's the stroke-prevention step of a stroke-prevention procedure. A tip that can shear off and stay in the artery risks the exact complication (embolism, stroke, TIA) the device exists to prevent. Layer a cyberattack disrupting shipping on top of a recall pulling product out of hospitals, and you get a supply crunch for a procedure without many easy substitutes; the stock dropped on the combined news.

Technical & Regulatory: FDA's notice updates a July 24 early alert to a full Class I recall, instructing facilities to stop use and distribution immediately, quarantine affected lots by number, and return stock to Boston Scientific; one serious injury had been reported as of July 9. Recall root-cause and CAPA (corrective/preventive action) work will run alongside the company's cybersecurity incident response — a reminder that quality and security incidents increasingly share the same manufacturing and logistics backbone.

Dexcom, Abbott Await Medicare Decision on CGM Coverage Expansion

Market Developments

Core Development: CMS is expected to decide by year-end whether to expand Medicare coverage of continuous glucose monitors to type 2 diabetics who don't use insulin — today's coverage is generally limited to insulin-dependent patients or those with a history of serious hypoglycemia. Dexcom and Abbott both flagged the pending decision this week, citing a randomized controlled trial presented at the American Diabetes Association's Scientific Sessions showing improved glucose control from CGM use in this broader population.

Context & Impact: This is a reimbursement story, but it's the kind that reshapes a market more than most product launches do. Dexcom estimates roughly 25 million Americans have non-insulin-treated type 2 diabetes, about half of them Medicare-age; Abbott's CEO called it "a multibillion-dollar opportunity" and put the newly-eligible Medicare population at around 10 million people. Coverage decisions, not FDA clearances, are usually the actual gate on whether a device reaches the patients who could benefit from it.

Technical & Regulatory: This runs through CMS's National Coverage Determination process rather than FDA — a decision is expected before year-end 2026, with implementation targeted for mid-2027, so it's a signal to watch rather than a done deal. Both companies are already building out sales and distribution capacity in anticipation, which is itself a bet on the outcome.
